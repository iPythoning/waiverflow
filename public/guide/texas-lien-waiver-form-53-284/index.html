The Texas Lien Waiver Form (Property Code §53.284)

Texas doesn't let you make up your own waiver wording either — Property Code §53.284 prescribes the exact form, in four versions. Here's which one to sign, and a field-by-field walkthrough of the one subs sign most often.

Before you start

Pick the right version first. If you haven't been paid for this period yet, use a conditional waiver — it only takes effect once the check clears. If the money has already landed, use an unconditional one. (Same logic as California — here's the 2-minute conditional vs. unconditional explainer →)

Step 1 — Pick which of the four §53.284 forms you need

Texas Property Code §53.284 sets out four statutory forms. They split two ways: conditional vs. unconditional (does it take effect when the check clears, or the instant you sign) and progress vs. final (does it release this billing period, or the whole job).

 The walkthrough below uses the conditional progress waiver, §53.284(b) — the one subs and suppliers sign most often. The other three ask for the same kind of details; the unconditional versions drop the conditioning-check fields because they take effect on signature.

Step 2 — Fill in the form, field by field

Here is every blank on the §53.284(b) conditional progress waiver, in the order WaiverFlow asks for them, and what each one means.

  1. Project

    The project name or address this waiver applies to. Be specific enough that the job is unmistakable — Texas waivers are tied to a particular project, not a blanket release.

  2. Job No.

    Your internal job or invoice number for this project. Optional, but it makes the waiver easy to match to the right billing later.

  3. Maker of Check · Amount of Check · Check Payable To — what makes it "conditional"

    On the conditional form, the release only becomes effective "on receipt by the signer of a check from (maker) in the sum of (amount) payable to (payee), and when the check has been properly endorsed and has been paid by the bank on which it is drawn." So you name that exact check. If it never clears, the waiver never takes effect — which is what protects you.

  4. Release Extent / Job Description

    What this progress payment covers — the labor, services, equipment, or materials you're releasing claims for. This defines the extent of the release, so tie it to the billing period the check pays, not the whole job.

  5. Person With Whom Signer Contracted

    The party you contracted with — usually the general contractor, or whoever hired you and is issuing the payment. The form releases claims for work furnished to the property or to this party.

  6. Company Name

    Your business's legal name — the claimant giving up the mechanic's lien and payment-bond claim rights.

  7. Signer Name · Title

    The name of the person signing and their title (owner, officer, or authorized agent of the company).

  8. Date

    The date of signature. That's the whole execution — see the note below on notarization.

No notary required. The statutory waiver forms under §53.284 don't need to be notarized. A conditional waiver is effective once the named check is endorsed and paid; an unconditional one is effective when signed — provided the form substantially follows the statutory text. That fixed wording is what makes the form self-executing.

The mistakes that void a Texas waiver

Watch these four

  • Signing an unconditional waiver before payment clears. An unconditional §53.284 waiver releases your rights the moment you sign — even if the check later bounces. Before the money is in your account, only ever sign a conditional waiver.
  • A Release Extent that's too broad. If the job description sweeps in more than this payment covers, you've released claims for work you haven't been paid for. Tie it to the invoice the check is paying.
  • Forgetting it covers bond claims too. The Texas form releases mechanic's lien rights and payment-bond claim rights. On a bonded job, signing waives both — so make sure the payment actually covers what you're releasing.
  • Rewriting the form text. Texas requires the waiver to substantially follow the §53.284 statutory wording. A waiver you drafted yourself, or pulled from another state, may not be enforceable here.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Texas lien waiver form under §53.284?

Texas Property Code §53.284 prescribes the statutory form for waiving and releasing a mechanic's lien or payment bond claim. There are four versions — conditional vs. unconditional, progress vs. final. To be effective, a Texas waiver must substantially track the statutory form text for the situation it covers.

How many Texas lien waiver forms are there?

Four: an unconditional progress waiver, a conditional progress waiver, an unconditional final waiver, and a conditional final waiver. Pick based on whether you've been paid yet (conditional vs. unconditional) and whether this is a periodic or final payment (progress vs. final).

Does a Texas lien waiver have to be notarized?

No. The statutory forms under §53.284 don't require notarization. A waiver is effective when signed — and, on the conditional forms, once the named check is paid — as long as it substantially follows the statutory form.

What's the difference between a conditional and unconditional Texas waiver?

A conditional waiver only becomes effective once the named check is endorsed and paid by the bank, so it's safe to sign before the money clears. An unconditional waiver releases your rights the moment you sign, regardless of whether you're actually paid — only sign it after payment has cleared.

Can I write my own Texas lien waiver language?

No. For a Texas waiver to be effective it must substantially comply with the statutory form in §53.284. A waiver drafted from scratch, or borrowed from another state, may not be enforceable. Fill in the blanks on the statutory form rather than rewriting it.
