How to Fill Out a California Lien Waiver, Step by Step

California won't let you write your own waiver language — the wording is fixed by statute and you just fill in the blanks. Here is every blank on the form, what to put in it, and the one field most people get wrong.

Before you start

Pick the right form first. If you have not been paid yet, use a conditional waiver. If the money has already cleared, use an unconditional one. (Not sure? Read the 2-minute conditional vs. unconditional explainer →)

Step 1 — Pick which of the four forms you need

California Civil Code prescribes four statutory waiver forms. They split two ways: conditional vs. unconditional (does it take effect on payment, or the instant you sign) and progress vs. final (does it release this billing period, or the whole job).

Step 2 — Fill in the form, field by field

Every California statutory waiver opens with the same identifying block. Here is what each line means.

  1. Name of Claimant

    Your business — the party giving up lien rights. The subcontractor, supplier, or laborer who is being paid. Use your legal business name, not a nickname or DBA you don't operate under.

  2. Name of Customer

    The party that hired you and is making the payment — usually the general contractor, or whoever you signed your contract with. This is not automatically the property owner.

  3. Job Location

    The project address — street address or legal description of the property where you did the work. Be specific enough that the project is unmistakable.

  4. Owner

    The property owner's name. If you're not sure, check your contract or the preliminary notice (20-day notice) you served at the start of the job.

  5. Through Date — the field people get wrong

    The cutoff date this waiver covers. It releases your lien rights for labor and materials billed through this date only — anything you do afterward stays protected. Match it to the billing period the payment covers. Set it too late and you've released rights for work you haven't been paid for.

  6. Maker of Check · Amount of Check · Check Payable To

    On a conditional waiver only. The conditional waiver is effective "only on the claimant's receipt of payment from the financial institution on which the following check is drawn." So you name the check: who it's drawn on, the amount, and the payee. If that check never clears, the waiver never takes effect. (The unconditional forms drop these fields, because they take effect on signature regardless.)

  7. Exceptions

    List anything you are not releasing: retentions being held, extras or change orders you haven't been paid for, and disputed amounts. Anything you leave off this line is released. The statute already excludes retentions, unpaid extras, and certain contract rights — but spell out specifics so there's no argument later.

  8. Claimant's Signature · Title · Date of Signature

    Sign as the claimant, add your title (owner, officer, or authorized agent), and date it. That's the whole execution — see the note below on notarization.

No notary required. The four California statutory waiver forms don't need to be notarized. A waiver is effective when the claimant signs it, as long as it substantially follows the statutory form text. That's the whole point of the fixed statutory language — it makes the form self-executing.

The mistakes that void a California waiver

Watch these four

  • Signing an unconditional waiver before payment clears. An unconditional waiver releases your rights the moment you sign — even if the check bounces. Before the money is in your account, only ever sign a conditional waiver.
  • A Through Date that's too late. If it runs past the period the payment actually covers, you've waived rights for unpaid work. Tie it to the invoice the check is paying.
  • Leaving retentions and extras off the Exceptions line. Unlisted amounts get released. If you're owed retention or have open change orders, name them.
  • Rewriting the form text. California requires the waiver to substantially follow the statutory wording. A waiver you drafted yourself, or pulled from another state, may not be enforceable here.

Frequently asked questions

What information do I need to fill out a California lien waiver?

For the conditional waiver on progress payment (§8132): the name of the claimant (your business), the name of the customer who hired you, the job location, the property owner, the through date, the maker/amount/payee of the check, any exceptions, and your signature, title and date.

What is the Through Date on a California lien waiver?

The cutoff date through which the waiver releases your lien rights. It only gives up rights for work billed through that date — anything afterward stays protected. Match it to the billing period the payment covers; setting it too late can release rights for unpaid work.

Does a California lien waiver need to be notarized?

No. The four statutory forms under Civil Code §§8132, 8134, 8136 and 8138 don't require notarization. They're effective when the claimant signs, as long as the waiver substantially follows the statutory form.

Who is the claimant versus the customer?

The claimant is you — the party giving up lien rights (subcontractor, supplier, laborer being paid). The customer is the party that hired you and is paying, usually the general contractor. Neither is the property owner, which is a separate field.

What goes in the Exceptions section?

Amounts you are NOT releasing: retentions being held, extras or change orders you haven't been paid for, and outstanding prior progress payments. Anything not listed as an exception is released, so review it carefully before signing.
