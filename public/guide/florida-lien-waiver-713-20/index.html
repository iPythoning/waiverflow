The Florida Lien Waiver Form (Fla. Stat. §713.20)

Florida is stricter than most states about waiver wording — and simpler. Statute §713.20 prescribes the exact form, but only two of them, and both are unconditional. Here's why that matters, which one to sign, and a field-by-field walkthrough.

Read this first

Florida is different from California and Texas. There is no conditional version in the core statutory forms — both the progress and final waivers take effect the moment you sign, paid or not. So the golden rule here is simple: don't sign a Florida statutory waiver until the payment has actually cleared your account.

Step 1 — Progress or final? (those are your only two choices)

Florida Statutes §713.20 sets out exactly two statutory forms. Unlike California and Texas — which each give you four (conditional vs. unconditional, progress vs. final) — Florida splits only one way: progress vs. final. Both are unconditional.

Step 2 — Fill in the progress waiver, field by field

Here is every blank on the §713.20(4) waiver and release upon progress payment, in the order WaiverFlow asks for them, and what each one means.

  1. Amount of Payment

    The dollar amount of the progress payment this waiver is given in consideration of. The form reads "in consideration of the sum of $______." This is the money you're acknowledging for this release.

  2. Through Date

    The date through which your labor, services, or materials are covered. The form waives the lien "for labor, services, or materials furnished through (this date)." Anything you furnish after it is not released — and neither is retention.

  3. Customer Name

    The name of your customer — the party you contracted with and are billing for this work.

  4. Owner

    The name of the owner of the job. The statutory form releases the lien "on the job of (owner)," so this identifies whose project the waiver applies to.

  5. Description of Property

    The property the lien attaches to. The form releases the lien "to the following property," so describe it clearly enough to be unmistakable — typically the address or legal description.

  6. Lienor

    The legal name of the lienor — your business, the party giving up the construction lien rights.

  7. By (print name and title/office) — who signs

    The name and title of the person signing on behalf of the lienor (owner, officer, or authorized agent).

  8. Dated on

    The date of signature. That completes the form — see the note below on notarization.

No notary, no witness required. The statutory waiver forms under §713.20 are effective when signed — Florida does not require them to be notarized or witnessed, as long as the form substantially follows the statutory text. That fixed wording is exactly why you fill in the blanks rather than rewrite it.

The mistakes that hurt you on a Florida waiver

Watch these four

  • Signing before the payment clears. This is the big one. Florida's statutory waivers are unconditional — there is no "takes effect when the check clears" safety net. If you sign and the check bounces, you may have released your lien rights anyway. Wait until the money is in your account.
  • Assuming the progress waiver covers retention. It doesn't. The §713.20(4) form expressly does not cover retention, or labor and materials furnished after the through-date. Those rights survive until the final waiver.
  • Using a non-statutory form. Florida is unusually strict: a waiver should substantially follow the §713.20 form, and a lienor cannot be required to use a different one. A custom or out-of-state form may not hold up.
  • A through-date that's later than the work this payment covers. The through-date defines what you're releasing. Set it past work you haven't been paid for and you've waived claims for free.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Florida lien waiver form under §713.20?

Florida Statutes §713.20 prescribes the statutory forms for waiving and releasing a construction lien. There are two: the waiver and release of lien upon progress payment under §713.20(4), and the waiver and release of lien upon final payment under §713.20(5). A lienor cannot be required to furnish a waiver different from these statutory forms.

How many Florida lien waiver forms are there?

Two. Unlike California and Texas, which each prescribe four forms, Florida §713.20 prescribes only two statutory waiver forms — one for progress payments and one for final payment. Both are unconditional and take effect when signed.

Are Florida lien waivers conditional or unconditional?

The two statutory forms under §713.20(4) and (5) are unconditional — they take effect the moment they're signed, regardless of whether the payment actually clears. Florida doesn't provide a conditional version in the core statutory forms, so don't sign a Florida statutory waiver until you've actually received and cleared the payment.

Does a Florida lien waiver have to be notarized?

No. The statutory waiver and release forms under §713.20 don't require notarization or a witness. The waiver is effective when signed, as long as it substantially follows the statutory form.

Does the Florida progress waiver release retention?

No. The §713.20(4) waiver and release upon progress payment expressly doesn't cover retention, or any labor, services, or materials furnished after the through-date you specify. Those rights survive until released separately or by the final waiver.
