Do I Need a Lien Waiver to Get Paid?

Short answer: on almost every commercial job, the GC or owner won't release your check until you hand over a signed lien waiver. It usually isn't the law forcing you — it's the payment process. Here's why, and how to sign one without ever giving up money you haven't actually been paid.

The 10-second answer

You're rarely legally required to sign — but if you don't, you don't get paid. So the real question isn't "do I have to," it's "which waiver do I sign, and when." Before the money's in your account, sign a conditional waiver — it doesn't release anything until the check actually clears. (2-minute conditional vs. unconditional explainer →)

Why payment is tied to a waiver in the first place

A mechanic's lien is the thing that gives subs and suppliers leverage: if you're not paid, you can put a claim on the property itself. That's great for you — and exactly what the owner and their lender are terrified of. They're releasing money down a chain of contractors they can't all see, and they need proof that each dollar they pay out actually clears the lien rights it's paying for.

The lien waiver is that proof. It's the receipt that says "this payment releases the lien rights it covers." So the GC collects a signed waiver from everyone below them for each payment, and the owner collects one from the GC, all the way up. No waiver, no clean title — so no check. That's why your pay application almost always comes back with a waiver to sign.

The catch: a waiver can release rights you haven't been paid for

Here's the trap. If you sign an unconditional waiver and hand it over with your invoice, you've released your lien rights the moment you signed — before the money arrives. If that check is slow, short, or never comes, you've already given up your best leverage to collect it.

The fix is the conditional waiver. It only takes effect once the named check is endorsed and paid by the bank. So you can submit it with your pay application — satisfying the "send a waiver to get paid" requirement — while keeping every right intact until the money actually clears. Same logic in California and Texas.

How to use a waiver to get paid — without getting burned

  1. Expect the request with your pay app

    On most commercial jobs the GC or owner requires a signed waiver before releasing each progress and final payment. It's routine — not a sign they're stalling you.

  2. Confirm the form is conditional — before the money clears

    If you haven't been paid yet, it must be a conditional waiver. It only takes effect once the named check is paid by the bank, so submitting it releases nothing until you actually have the money.

  3. Generate the correct statutory form

    Pick your state and the matching form — California has four (§8132 / §8134 / §8136 / §8138); Texas has four under §53.284. The waiver must follow the statutory wording to be enforceable, so don't freehand it.

  4. Submit it with your invoice

    Send the signed conditional waiver alongside your pay application. That satisfies the payment condition while your lien rights stay intact until the check clears.

  5. Sign unconditional only after the check clears

    If they later ask for an unconditional waiver as a "paid receipt," that's normal — but only sign it once the payment has actually landed and cleared your account.

The one rule that matters. "Do I need a waiver to get paid" almost always means "they won't pay until I sign one." Fine — just make it conditional until the money's in your account. That single choice is the difference between a waiver that gets you paid and one that costs you.

The mistakes that actually cost subs money

Watch these three

  • Signing unconditional before payment clears. The single most expensive waiver mistake. You release your lien and bond rights on signature — if the check is short or never comes, your leverage is already gone. Send conditional pre-payment, every time.
  • A release that's too broad. If the waiver covers more work than this specific payment pays for, you've released claims for work you haven't been paid for. Tie each waiver to the exact invoice and period the payment covers.
  • Using a random "lien waiver" template. In California and Texas the waiver must substantially follow the statutory form to be enforceable. A generic template — or one borrowed from another state — may not hold up. Use the statutory form for your state.

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to sign a lien waiver to get paid?

On most commercial jobs, practically yes. The GC, owner, or lender almost always conditions each progress and final payment on a signed waiver from everyone below them. It usually isn't a law that forces you to sign — but if you refuse, the paying party can hold the payment. The safe move is to sign a conditional waiver, which only takes effect once you're actually paid.

Why do contractors and owners require a waiver before paying?

A mechanic's lien lets unpaid subs and suppliers claim against the property. Owners and lenders want proof that the money they release actually clears those lien rights, so the property stays free of claims. Requiring a waiver for each payment is how they get that proof — the waiver is the receipt that says "this payment releases the lien rights it covers."

Is it safe to sign a waiver before I receive the money?

Only if it's a conditional waiver. A conditional waiver doesn't take effect until the named check is endorsed and paid by the bank, so you can hand it over with your invoice without giving up anything until the money clears. Never sign an unconditional waiver before payment has landed — it releases your rights the moment you sign, even if the check bounces or never arrives.

Who's supposed to prepare the waiver — me or the party paying me?

Either can, but the claimant — the sub or supplier giving up the rights — signs it and is responsible for making sure it's the correct form and doesn't release more than the payment covers. Many paying parties send their own form; you're free to use the correct statutory form instead, which in California and Texas you have to substantially follow anyway.

What happens if I sign the wrong type of waiver?

The expensive mistake is signing an unconditional waiver before you're paid: you release your lien and bond rights immediately, and if the payment never clears you've given up your leverage to collect. The other common error is a release that's too broad — covering more work than the payment pays for. Match the form to your situation and tie the release to the specific payment.
