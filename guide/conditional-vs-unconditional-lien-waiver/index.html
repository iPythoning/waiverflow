Conditional vs. Unconditional Lien Waivers: Which One to Sign, and When

If you sign the wrong one at the wrong time, you can release your right to get paid before the money ever shows up. Here is the difference in one sentence, a side-by-side table, and exactly which form to use on a California or Texas job.

The one rule

A conditional waiver only takes effect once you are actually paid — sign it before the check clears. An unconditional waiver is binding the instant you sign it — sign it only after the money is in your account.

What a conditional waiver actually does

A conditional waiver and release says, in effect: "I give up my lien rights for this payment — but only if and when I actually receive it." The release is conditioned on payment. If the check never clears, the waiver never takes effect and your mechanics-lien rights stay fully intact.

This is why the conditional waiver is the one you hand to a general contractor or owner to get the check in the first place. You are not taking a risk: you have only agreed to release rights for money you are about to receive.

What an unconditional waiver actually does

An unconditional waiver and release releases your lien rights immediately and with no strings attached. It does not matter whether the payment cleared, bounced, or never came. Once you sign, those rights are gone.

The mistake that costs subs the most

A GC asks you to sign an unconditional waiver before handing over the check — "just so we can cut payment." If you sign, you have released your lien rights for a payment you do not yet have. If that check is late, short, or never arrives, you have no leverage left.

The fix is simple: before payment, only ever sign a conditional waiver. Sign the unconditional version after the funds have cleared your account.

Progress payment vs. final payment

On top of conditional vs. unconditional, each waiver is also tied to how much of the job it releases:

  • Progress payment — releases your lien rights only up through a specific date or billing period. You keep your rights for everything you do afterward. These are the waivers you sign throughout a multi-month job.
  • Final payment — releases all your remaining lien rights on the project. You sign this one when you have been paid in full and the job is closed out.

That gives four combinations — and California writes a separate statutory form for each.

The four California statutory forms (and Texas)

California does not let you write your own waiver language. Civil Code §§8132, 8134, 8136, and 8138 prescribe the exact wording for each of the four forms. A waiver that does not substantially follow the statutory form may not be effective.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a conditional and an unconditional lien waiver?

A conditional waiver only becomes effective once payment has actually cleared, so it is safe to sign before you have been paid. An unconditional waiver is effective the moment you sign it, whether or not payment ever arrives — sign it only after the payment has cleared.

When should I sign an unconditional lien waiver?

Only after the payment it covers has actually cleared — the check has deposited and the funds are available, or the wire has landed. Signing one earlier can leave you with no security if the payment bounces or never comes.

Is it safe to sign a conditional lien waiver before I get paid?

Generally yes. A conditional waiver only takes effect upon actual receipt of payment, so if the payment never clears, it does not release your lien rights. That is exactly why GCs exchange a conditional waiver for the check, and an unconditional waiver afterward.

What is the difference between a progress and a final payment waiver?

A progress-payment waiver releases lien rights only up through a specific date, so you keep your rights for later work. A final-payment waiver releases all remaining lien rights on the project. California has separate statutory forms for each.

Which lien waiver form does California require?

California Civil Code §§8132, 8134, 8136, and 8138 prescribe four statutory forms: conditional and unconditional on progress payment (8132, 8134) and on final payment (8136, 8138). The text is set by statute, and a non-conforming waiver may not be effective.
